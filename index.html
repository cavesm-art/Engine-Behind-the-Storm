Big idea 1: Warm air rises

Heat air and its molecules speed up and spread out. The air gets less dense. Less dense air is lighter. It rises.

Cool air is the opposite. It gets more dense. It is heavier. It sinks.

Big idea 2: Pressure makes wind

Where warm air rises, it leaves low pressure at the ground. Fewer molecules press down.

Where cool air sinks, it piles up. That is high pressure.

Air always flows from high pressure to low pressure. That flow is wind. A bigger pressure difference means stronger wind.

Big idea 3: Convection and latent heat

A convection current is a loop. Warm air rises. It cools at the top. It sinks. It gets heated again. The loop keeps going as long as there is heat at the bottom.

Rising air cools. Water vapor in it condenses into clouds. Condensing releases latent heat. That heat makes the air rise even faster. A storm is a convection loop stuck in overdrive.

What is happening

On a hot, humid day the sun heats the ground. The air near the ground warms up. It gets less dense. It shoots upward. That is the updraft.

As the air rises it cools. Its water vapor condenses into cloud. Condensing releases latent heat. That heat pushes the updraft even harder.

The rising air leaves low pressure at the ground. Air rushes in from all sides to fill the gap. That inflow feeds the storm. Taller, faster updraft means a more violent storm.

updraft: a column of air moving up fast
latent heat: heat released when water vapor condenses
anvil: the flat top of a tall storm cloud

What is happening

Tornadoes form inside supercells, the strongest thunderstorms. Wind that changes speed and direction with height is called wind shear. Shear makes the rising air start to rotate. A rotating updraft is a mesocyclone.

The spinning column stretches down toward the ground. As it tightens, the pressure in the middle drops very low. Air rushes in toward that low-pressure core. The closer it gets, the faster it spins, like a skater pulling in their arms.

wind shear: wind speed or direction changing as you go up
mesocyclone: a rotating updraft inside a supercell
core: the center of the spinning column; the lowest pressure

What is happening

When ocean water is at least 26°C (79°F), it evaporates fast. The air above it fills with warm moisture. That air rises in huge columns. A deep area of low pressure forms at the surface.

Air spirals in toward the center. It spins faster as it gets closer. The center is the eye. Air sinks there, so it is calm. The ring around the eye, the eyewall, has the strongest convection and the strongest winds.

Over cool water or land, the fuel is gone. The storm weakens.

eye: the calm center where air sinks
eyewall: the ring of strongest wind and rising air
evaporation: liquid water turning into vapor (gas)

What is happening

A low-pressure system keeps pulling in warm, moist air. That feeds convection and rain over the same spot, again and again.

A flash flood happens when a strong convective storm drops a huge amount of rain fast. The ground cannot soak it up. The water runs off into streams in minutes.

A river flood builds slowly. A low-pressure system sends storm after storm over the same area for days. The rivers fill up past what they can hold.

runoff: rain water that flows over the ground instead of soaking in
watershed: all the land that drains into one river

What is happening

In a high-pressure system, air is sinking, not rising. Sinking air squeezes together and warms up. Warm, sinking air dries out moisture instead of letting it condense.

No rising air means no convection. No convection means no clouds and no rain. Just clear skies and sun, day after day.

The longer the high-pressure dome stays, the drier the land gets. That is a drought: a convection shutdown.

dome: a big, stuck area of high pressure
suppress: to hold down or stop something

What is happening

Winter storms come from big pressure differences. Cold Arctic air is dense and sinking (high pressure). Warmer air to the south has lower pressure. When a strong low pulls the cold air south, the two air masses crash together. That makes heavy snow, ice, and blizzards.

Lake-effect snow is a tiny convection story. Cold, dense air blows over the warm Great Lakes. The lake heats the air above it. That warmed, moist air rises. It cools, condenses, and freezes. Narrow bands of heavy snow fall on the downwind shore. Same physics as a thunderstorm, with ice instead of rain.

air mass: a huge blob of air with one temperature
downwind: the side the wind blows toward
pressure gradient: how fast pressure changes from place to place
